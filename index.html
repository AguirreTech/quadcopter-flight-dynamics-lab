Motor Mixer

Learning objective: Explore how differential thrust produces pitch, roll, yaw, and vertical motion.

Predict before experimenting: Which motors should increase thrust to initiate forward pitch?
The rear motors increase thrust relative to the front, creating a nose-down pitching moment. Once the target attitude is reached, the controller changes the motor balance again.

LESSON 02 · LEARNING GUIDE

Tilt & Thrust

Learning objective: Investigate how tilting redirects total thrust into vertical and horizontal components.

Predict before experimenting: At 30° tilt, will the aircraft need more or less total thrust to maintain altitude?
More. In the ideal model, T = mg / cos(30°), approximately 1.155 times hover thrust. The vertical component still balances weight.
Instructor discussion: Ask students to identify which variables are commanded, which are measured, and what assumptions the model makes. Compare the visual result with a real aircraft demonstration.
Model boundary: The force model neglects aerodynamic lift and other vertical forces.

Why does a tilted drone need more thrust?

Think of the blue arrow as the total push from the propellers. When the drone tilts, some of that push goes sideways instead of holding it up. Use right stick up for forward tilt, right stick down for backward tilt, and left stick up/down to change the strength of the push.

Required for level altitude: T = mg / cos θ. Actual vertical component: T cos θ. Signed horizontal component: T sin θ (forward positive, backward negative).

Idealized direct-thrust model: the throttle stick is mapped to 0–200% of level-hover thrust, with midpoint representing 100%. Real altitude-hold flight controllers interpret throttle differently. Pitch stick is mapped to the displayed tilt for teaching purposes, not as a physical instantaneous response.

2 · Tune the roll controller

PID stands for Proportional, Integral, and Derivative. A PID controller uses feedback to reduce the difference (error) between the commanded roll rate and the measured roll rate. P (Proportional) responds to the current error; I (Integral) accumulates error over time to correct persistent bias; and D (Derivative) responds to how quickly the measured motion changes, helping reduce overshoot and oscillation.

Change P, I, or D, then observe the live correction and motor outputs immediately beside the sliders. Pitch is a separate illustrative response and is not tuned here.

PID gains · adjust and compare

Engineering model: In Angle mode an outer attitude loop sets a target roll rate. In both modes an inner PID rate loop controls roll angular velocity. Derivative is applied to measured rate (with a simple filter), rather than taking a raw derivative of stick commands.

Engineering detail · simplified feedback model

Angle mode: stick position requests a roll angle; a proportional outer loop generates a target roll rate. Acro/Rate mode: stick position directly requests a roll rate. In both modes, a damped inner rate loop drives roll acceleration. The simulated gyroscope reports roll rate, not roll angle or position. The model uses ideal measurements, an illustrative disturbance and artificial damping. It is not DJI, Betaflight, or a certified flight simulator.

Angle: ωtarget = Kangle(φtarget − φ)
Rate: ωtarget = stick × ωmax
Motor moment: τ ≈ Krate(ωtarget − ω)
Rotation: I φ̈ = τ

Instructor note: Angle mode self-levels attitude, not position. Acro/Rate mode stabilizes angular rate, but does not automatically level. Neither mode guarantees altitude or position hold. This visualization is intentionally limited to roll dynamics.

Engineering detail: the simplified feedback model
Angle mode: desired rate = clamp(Kangle × (desired roll angle − estimated roll angle))
Acro mode: desired rate = stick × maximum roll rate
Rate error = desired rate − gyro rate
Roll control = clamp(Krate × rate error)
Motor commands = collective thrust ± roll control
Angular acceleration ∝ motor-generated roll moment + disturbance torque

For teaching clarity, this model uses a single roll axis, ideal sensors, proportional control, and simplified rigid-body response. Motor Failure

Learning objective: Explore motor effectiveness, command limits, and the limits of control authority.

Predict before experimenting: If one motor loses 25% effectiveness, can the quadcopter always maintain balanced hover?
Not always. Compensation depends on remaining thrust headroom and whether all required moments can be balanced simultaneously. A complete failure generally prevents conventional fully controlled hover.
Instructor discussion: Ask students to identify which variables are commanded, which are measured, and what assumptions the model makes. Compare the visual result with a real aircraft demonstration.
Model boundary: Static control allocation is not a prediction of a real aircraft's recovery trajectory.

The solver minimizes weighted errors in collective lift, roll moment, pitch moment, and yaw reaction torque subject to each motor's command limit. It can sacrifice yaw authority to preserve other objectives. No stable-flight guarantee follows from this snapshot.

4 · Predict → Test → Explain

Challenge: With 160% motor command available, can a motor operating at 75% effectiveness still produce its original hover thrust? What changes at 0% effectiveness? Does prioritizing yaw alter the trade-off?

A 75%-effective motor requires 100/0.75 = 133.3% of its original hover command to generate the same thrust. If that command is within the available range, the ideal four-motor hover balance can still be achieved.

When one rotor produces no thrust, four independent hover objectives (lift, roll, pitch, yaw) generally cannot all be satisfied with the three remaining rotors. Depending on priorities, a controller may trade yaw control against other objectives. A complete failure does not imply a specific trajectory in this static model. Specialized fault-tolerant controllers may maintain limited flight while sacrificing yaw, but that is not normal consumer quadcopter behavior.

Engineering details · control allocation
Actual normalized thrust: fᵢ = ηᵢ uᵢ, with 0 ≤ uᵢ ≤ uₘₐₓ.
Target: Σ fᵢ = 4 hover units; roll = 0; pitch = 0; yaw reaction torque = 0.
Roll moment ∝ (f₁ + f₃) − (f₂ + f₄).
Pitch moment ∝ (f₃ + f₄) − (f₁ + f₂).
Yaw reaction torque ∝ −f₁ + f₂ + f₃ − f₄, under the assumed rotor directions.
Bounded weighted least squares minimizes the control residual. Normalized torque coefficients and motor-to-thrust mapping are simplified.
